# DXY

The US Dollar Index (DXY) measures the US dollar against a fixed, weighted basket of six major currencies. It is a critical macro indicator: dollar moves feed into commodities, precious metals, emerging markets and US company earnings. The handbook note lists the dollar as a driver of gold ("rates low or dollar weaker = gold more attractive") and DXY as a driver of silver (from [[Markets - Handbook]]). The daily brief template opens its FX read with "USD broad" and checks dollar liquidity in the regime call (from [[Markets - Brief]]).

## What DXY is

| Currency | Weight |
| --- | --- |
| Euro (EUR) | 57.6% |
| Japanese yen (JPY) | 13.6% |
| British pound (GBP) | 11.9% |
| Canadian dollar (CAD) | 9.1% |
| Swedish krona (SEK) | 4.2% |
| Swiss franc (CHF) | 3.6% |

- **Calculation**: 100 × the geometric weighted average of each rate's change from the base period. The base is 100, set in March 1973. In full:

```
DXY = 50.14348112 × EURUSD^-0.576 × USDJPY^0.136 × GBPUSD^-0.119 × USDCAD^0.091 × USDSEK^0.042 × USDCHF^0.036
```

- **Euro-heavy**: with the euro at more than half the basket, DXY moves largely as the inverse of EUR/USD. China and Mexico are not in the basket.
- **Range**: typically 90–110 in recent decades; extremes are rare. The all-time extremes were about 165 (February 1985) and about 71 (March 2008).

## Macro implications

- **Strong DXY (>105)**: risk-off, high US rates, global stress.
- **Weak DXY (<95)**: risk-on, low US rates, growth-positive.
- **DXY at extremes**: often precedes reversals, reverting towards the mean of 100.

## Cross-asset effects

- **Commodities**: inverse relationship. Commodities are priced in dollars, so a weak dollar means higher dollar prices for commodities. See [[Commodities]].
- **Gold and silver**: inverse relationship. A weak dollar makes gold cheaper for buyers outside the US, which makes it more attractive. See [[Gold]] and [[Silver]].
- **EM currencies**: weak dollar = EM strength; strong dollar = EM weakness.
- **Equity earnings**: a strong dollar means weaker international earnings once translated into USD. See [[Equities]].

## Interest rate sensitivity

- **US rates rising above global rates**: DXY strengthens on the higher relative return.
- **US rates falling below global rates**: DXY weakens on the lower relative return.

Rate differentials and real yields are covered in [[Rates]] and [[Real Yields]].

## Trading implications

- **Dollar strength plays**: a strong DXY is good for USD borrowers, US exporters in real terms, and US rates relative to global rates.
- **Dollar weakness plays**: a weak DXY is good for commodities, EM equities, precious metals and importers.
- **Carry trades**: a weak dollar enables carry trades (borrow cheap USD, invest globally).
- **Carry unwind**: a strong dollar forces carry trades to unwind ("yen carry especially brutal").

Correction, 15 October 2026: standard FX logic reverses three of these lines. A strong dollar makes US exports dearer abroad and imports cheaper, so it favours US importers, not exporters; a weak dollar favours US exporters. A strong dollar raises the burden for anyone who owes dollars but earns in another currency. A yen carry trade borrows yen, so it unwinds when the yen strengthens, which is dollar weakness against the yen; a strong dollar squeezes dollar-funded carry instead.

## Levels and technicals

- **100**: psychological anchor and mean.
- **105**: extreme strength; rarely sustained.
- **95**: extreme weakness; rare.
- **Support and resistance**: previous highs and lows create levels.
