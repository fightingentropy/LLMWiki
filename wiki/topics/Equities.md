# Equities

Equities are the riskiest major asset class and the most sensitive to growth expectations. The daily brief reads them through "index internals, breadth, leadership, cyclicals vs defensives" ([[Markets - Brief]]). Saved stock theses and setups live in [[Bookmarks - Markets & Crypto]]; the AI valuation debate is on [[AI Infrastructure Economics]].

## Breadth and leadership

- **Breadth:** the advance/decline ratio shows how many stocks join a move. A few names carrying the index is concentration risk, and deteriorating breadth warns of weakness.
- **Leadership:** gains concentrated in 7–10 mega-cap tech names are fragile. Cyclicals beating defensives means the market expects growth; defensives leading means it fears a slowdown.

## Sector rotation

Leadership shifts between sectors as growth expectations and the regime change.

- **Cyclicals:** industrials, materials, energy, financials, consumer discretionary.
- **Defensives:** staples, healthcare, utilities, real estate.
- **Mixed:** technology (capex boom or caution), telecom (bond-like), energy transition.

| Phase | Leaders | Laggards |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early cycle | Cyclicals, especially industrials and materials | Defensives |
| Mid-cycle | Broad strength; tech and growth as capex accelerates | Few |
| Late cycle | Energy and materials (inflation) | Long-duration growth, rate-sensitive defensives |
| Recession | Defensives, staples | Cyclicals |

Three ways to trade it: ride the rotation already under way (momentum); fade the cyclical/defensive or value/growth ratio at extremes (mean reversion); or follow the data (accelerating PMIs favour cyclicals, widening credit spreads favour defensives). Watch the cyclical/defensive ratio, earnings revisions, economic surprises and Fed tone.

Crypto has its own sector rotation, with one caution from [[Rewkang]] about markets fed by new money:

> "Traders that short memecoins because they are “being rotated out of” eventually will get blown out just as those that faded AXS, ADA, LUNA last cycle" (from [[Crypto Takes]])

## Valuation

A three-layer split, credited to Arbor Research via @MacroAlf:

| Layer | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| What's real | Book value |
| What's likely | Discounted earnings, next 3 years |
| Hopes & Dreams | Everything else |

> "Way to go before Hopes & Dreams turn into Despair & Nightmares."

Quality comes before price: "it's far better to buy a bussin company at a sus price than a sus company at a bussin price, fr fr". The entry price still sets the margin of safety. Compare the earnings yield with bond yields to judge relative value ([[Rates and Credit]]).

## Rates and growth

High-growth stocks behave like long-duration bonds: when rates or real yields rise, their multiples compress even if earnings hold. Price leads fundamentals: "Stocks peak when the best has already been discounted, with fundamentals often deteriorating later as expectations fail to match reality." (from [[Business & Finance]])

## Flows and the calendar

- **Buybacks** support prices; **index rebalancing** brings scheduled flows; **options expiry** creates gamma-hedging flows.
- **The calendar:** "For the U.S. markets the year really begins in September." Volume stays strong to Christmas and again from January to May ("Sell in May and Go Away"). Late August and the week after July 4 are the quietest. FOMC days bring "wild market gyrations, especially just after the committee releases its statement". Low volume "can often lead to unexpected market movements that don't follow typical patterns." Session times are on [[Exchange Microstructure]].

Correction (15 October 2026): an earlier version said activity is strong from January to October and that most annual moves happen from late August to December; the source says neither.

## Recession signals

Falling forward earnings estimates, widening credit spreads, rising unemployment and compressing multiples. Credit usually moves first ([[Rates and Credit]]).

## How bear markets unfold

Robert Rhea (1935), via @WifeyAlpha:

> "Bear markets seem to be divided into three phases: the first being the abandonment of hopes upon which the uprush of the preceding bull market was predicated; the second being the reflection of the decreased earning power and reduction of dividends; the third representing distress liquidation of securities which must be sold to meet living expenses."

Each phase is separated by a bear-market rally "which is often erroneously assumed to be the beginning of a bull market."
