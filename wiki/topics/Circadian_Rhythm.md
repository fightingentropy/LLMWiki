# Circadian Rhythm

The circadian rhythm is the body's roughly 24-hour clock. It sets the daily timing of sleep, alertness and hormones such as melatonin and cortisol. In Erlin's notes it connects [[ADHD]], [[Body_Optimization|Body Optimization]] and [[Mental_Health|Mental Health]]. The strongest material is a 2025 paper arguing that a large subgroup of people with ADHD run on a delayed body clock. The rest is a handful of lines from personal notes about sleep timing, sunlight and bedtime habits, kept here as viewpoints to investigate rather than prescriptions.

## ADHD and the delayed body clock

Nothing in this section is a diagnosis or a treatment plan; sleep and ADHD concerns belong with a clinician.

Erlin's medical note saves the line "We recently reconceptualized ADHD as a circadian rhythm disorder." beside a [post by Nicholas Fabiano](https://x.com/NTFabiano/status/2020162108919357483), co-author of the paper. (from [[Medical|Medical & Health]])

Brandon Luu's [thread on ADHD and delayed body clocks](https://x.com/BrandonLuuMD/status/2102338491942347175), the [explanation it quotes](https://x.com/anishmoonka/status/2102284259553890448), and the [Luu and Fabiano 2025 perspective](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1697900/full) say (from [[Bookmarks - Health & Psychology]]):

- People with ADHD tend to have later biological sleep/wake timing.
- The paper cites delayed sleep/wake timing in up to 78%, sleep disturbances in up to 80% of adults and 82% of children, and an evening melatonin rise about 45 minutes later in children and 90 minutes later in adults.
- The thread's graphic depicts later melatonin timing and a later, flatter morning cortisol rise. Those hormone curves are conceptual illustrations, not individual measurements.
- Early evidence suggests that shifting the body clock earlier may ease symptoms for some people.
- The proposed approach: screen for sleep and circadian problems; keep a consistent wake-up time; get morning bright light; cut evening light and screen exposure; keep regular routines; use selective low-dose melatonin when delayed timing is identified.
- The authors describe these measures as additions to standard ADHD care, with treatment evidence still developing.

What the paper actually supports is narrower than the medical-note line. It describes a circadian phenotype in a subgroup, not ADHD as a whole, and its treatment discussion is adjunctive. [[ADHD]] carries the explicit correction: the paper rejects exclusive reclassification of ADHD as a circadian disorder.

A separate anecdotal [thread by @ArsAlendi](https://x.com/ArsAlendi/status/2096617680815980866) treats ADHD as partly a histamine and sleep issue after one reported response to an antihistamine. It does not establish a general mechanism or a safe treatment. (from [[Bookmarks - Health & Psychology]])

## Sleep timing and sleep length

- Erlin's body note: "sleeping late and waking up late brain fog." The note links late sleep and late waking to brain fog; it does not compare timing against duration. (from [[Body]])
- A [YouTube video saved at 53:22](https://youtu.be/hQ78Hpwrt80?t=3202) under the line "Reason why you can't sleep at night." (from [[Body]])
- Tim Urban's [two-hour sleep thought experiment](https://x.com/waitbutwhy/status/1906866707534147681) asks why people give up two hours of sleep when they would eagerly take a drug promising the benefits of two extra hours. It is a behavioural prompt, not a measured comparison. [NIH guidance](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep/how-much-sleep) says adults generally need seven to nine hours, and individual needs vary. (from [[Bookmarks - Health & Psychology]])

## Light, sunlight and dopamine

In the circadian material, light matters as a timing cue: morning bright light and less evening light are part of the Luu and Fabiano approach above.

The medical note makes a different, biochemical claim: "Dopamine is synthesized from the amino acid Tyrosine, The first and crucial step is made by the enzyme Tyrosine Hydroxylase, Sunlight is needed for this step." (from [[Medical|Medical & Health]])

**Correction, 22 September 2026:** the tyrosine-hydroxylase reaction uses tyrosine, oxygen and tetrahydrobiopterin. Sunlight is not a reagent in that reaction ([NCBI PubChem catecholamine biosynthesis pathway](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pathway/BioCyc%3AHUMAN_PWY66-301); [original enzyme kinetics study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6117320/)). Effects of light exposure on circadian timing are a separate question. (from [[Health Evidence Review - September 2026]])

## Bedtime supplements and cold exposure

These are attributed lines from notes, not treatment advice; check supplements with a clinician or pharmacist.

- **Magnesium before bed.** The body note, next to the handle @hubermanlab (see [[Andrew Huberman]]): "Vitamin D3 supplementation: largely accepted nowadays. Creatine: starting to break through. Next: Magnesium (Threonate or Bisglycinate). Why? Well for starters, Mg can protect against hearing loss, solid data on reducing migraines & it can improve sleep." Timing: "30-60min before bed." The notes supply no controlled evidence for a universal magnesium sleep, hearing and migraine stack. See [[Supplements|Supplements & Biohacking]]. (from [[Body]], [[Health Evidence Review - September 2026]])
- **Tongkat Ali and sleep.** "Also don’t take tongkat Ali too late in the afternoon because it can disrupt sleep." (from [[Body]])
- **Cold exposure.** The notes hold anecdotes that cold showers lift mood and release dopamine, not evidence that cold exposure resets the body clock. See [[Stress Management]] and [[Fitness]].

## What the notes cannot settle

**Correction, 22 September 2026:** an earlier version of this page presented "sleep timing > sleep duration," sunlight as a requirement for dopamine synthesis, bedtime magnesium, and cold exposure as settled mechanisms or a general treatment stack. Those claims were stronger than the evidence in the cited personal notes. They remain viewpoints to investigate, not prescriptions.

Sleep timing, light exposure, medication effects and symptoms are separate questions. A bookmark alone cannot establish an individual's cause or treatment. [[Health Evidence]] records the standard used when adding clinical claims.
