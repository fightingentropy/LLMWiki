# AI Infrastructure Economics

AI infrastructure economics asks whether spending on chips, memory, data centres, power and model training will earn its return, and who keeps the margin. The saved essays hold both the infrastructure bull case and the valuation bear case. Most are long posts on X, kept in [[Bookmarks - Markets & Crypto]], [[Bookmarks - AI & Agents]] and [[Bookmarks - Developer Tools]]. The practical side of agents is on [[AI Agents]]; the stock-market side on [[Equities]].

## Demand: users times tokens per user

- Compute demand grows through two separate variables: more users, and more tokens per user. Fin's essay "AI Semiconductor Endgame 2026 (III)" adds that coding-agent tools may spread from programmers into ordinary knowledge work.
- Adoption is a distribution, not a login count. Vas describes enterprise rollouts as a barbell: a few power users, a larger shallow group, and many who barely use it. Measure skill, workflow integration, verified output and return per token, not activations.
- Usage can come from features nobody notices. One user found Codex auto-review made up a large share of his calls and uncached tokens.
- The evidence that matters is paid workflows, completed tasks, integration cost, and usage that lasts beyond experiments.

## Cost and valuation

Large compute commitments are visible years before the revenue that would justify them. The bear arguments:

- **The cost ratchet.** Andrew Ho: competition forces repeated training spend, cheaper models cap prices, and slow application discovery delays the revenue that trillion-dollar valuations need.
- **Overbuild.** Fin expects eventual overbuilding and margin pressure even as demand grows, and open models moving profit around without cutting total compute.
- **Slower progress.** Quantian: if frontier capability gains flatten sharply, AI equity valuations need a severe reset.
- **No broad productivity gain.** Jeffrey Lee Funk: the gains stay in hyperscalers and semiconductors.
- **Disappointment into crypto.** [[goodalexander]] maps weak AI returns, hacking, addictive media and job losses to fiscal stress, stablecoins, tokenized debt and leveraged on-chain markets.
- **Speculation moved.** [[CryptoCred]] explains why speculative capital left crypto for AI: institutionalization, extraction, lost inefficiency and AI's opportunity cost.

Citrini Research's April 2025 "AI Phase 2" primer moved the question from scarce infrastructure to application adoption and monetization, with defensibility running from data and distribution to design to deployment ([[Macro Trading]]).

## Chips, memory and hardware

- **Memory cycles.** Jukan asks whether memory shares can peak three or four quarters before memory prices, as announced fab capacity comes into view. Track capex, clean-room and wafer-start timing, product mix, utilization, contract prices, and whether HBM's larger wafer needs absorb the new capacity. SK hynix's own capacity statements support tracking the supply response, not the timing call.
- **Accelerator lock-in.** Gavin Baker: different topologies, memory systems and networking reward model-hardware co-design and raise switching costs, as labs optimise tokens per watt per dollar.
- **Cheaper inference memory.** John Carmack sketches an accelerator that keeps weights in flash, with sequential NAND reads and a small HBM tier; it suits inference better than training.
- **Specialised silicon.** Taalas launched hardware built for inference.

## Power and data centres

Data-centre economics depend on electricity: generation, transmission, interconnection queues, congestion, contracts, regional prices and delivery timing. A primer by a former power-and-gas quant puts these at the centre of the buildout question. Inside the building, a low-cost networking simulator game teaches racks, cabling, traffic and bottlenecks.

## Speculative branches

- @izakaminska argues efficient models could end hyperscaling and push liquidity toward US Treasuries.
- @nic_carter, labelling it speculation, suggests Anthropic's biology lab and safety messaging could let it keep discoveries private or delay public access.

## Questions to track

- Which workflows generate recurring paid demand?
- Can cash generation keep pace with training, serving and capacity commitments?
- Where do power, data, integration and verification bottlenecks remain?
- Who captures value as model costs fall: labs, infrastructure, applications or users?
- When does capacity turn into overbuild and margin pressure?
