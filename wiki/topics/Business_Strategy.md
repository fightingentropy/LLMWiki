# Business Strategy

Business strategy here is a set of borrowed frameworks rather than one method: competition introduced on purpose, distinctiveness that has to be paid for continuously, profits that eat the customer, and incentives that decide outcomes. Most of it comes from [[Business & Finance]]; the startup and founder links are in [[Bookmarks - Business & Startups]].

## The catfish effect

The catfish effect (鲶鱼效应) is a Chinese business and policy idea: add a deliberate competitor so a stagnant system starts moving again. @ruima lays it out.

- **Origin.** Norwegian fishermen carried sardines in tanks; many stopped moving and died. One live catfish kept them swimming, and more survived. "The catfish made the environment slightly hostile, which weirdly made it more survivable."
- **Managed agitation.** Not disruption as a natural market event, but "let's introduce a little stress on purpose, because stagnation is the bigger risk here." In China competition "can be curated, introduced, even welcomed -- not to destroy the domestic players, but to get them moving again."
- **Tesla in China.** Frustrated with a subsidy-fed EV sector full of "half-baked companies just trying to cash in on government money", the government gave Tesla full ownership of its China operations, fast-tracked factory deals, land and logistics. The message to domestic makers: "here comes the catfish. Swim!" Innovation sped up afterwards, though "it wasn't all because of Tesla".
- **Limits.** "Sometimes the catfish is too strong, or the sardines were already too weak. And sometimes you get eaten, LOL."
- **The personal question.** "Are you swimming because you want to, or because you finally have to?"

The same mechanism works at other scales: a high performer dropped into a complacent team, or acute stress in the body, which @NoahRyanCo says "makes you better, stronger, more adaptive" while chronic stress wears you down ([[Body Optimization]]).

## Distinctiveness is continuous work

Jeff Bezos's last shareholder letter as Amazon's CEO, "Differentiation is Survival and the Universe Wants You to be Typical", opens with Richard Dawkins: a living body stays different from its surroundings only by constant work, and when the work stops it falls into equilibrium, which is death. Bezos applies it to companies, institutions and lives.

- "The world wants you to be typical - in a thousand ways, it pulls at you. Don't let it happen."
- "You have to pay a price for your distinctiveness, and it's worth it."
- "Democracies are not normal. Tyranny is the historical norm."

Curtis Yarvin's "Fundamentally it's entropy versus extropy" makes the same point about organised complexity ([[Philosophy & Meaning]]).

## Overearning

A thread replying to @buccocapital diagnoses Airbnb's fading brand as a "classic situation where fundamentals deteriorate with improving financials": the company is overearning by taking the whole consumer surplus. Another reply adds the regulatory risk of scaling through legal loopholes and changing policy later. Pricing at zero is the opposite failure ([[Economics]]).

## Speed: growing versus protecting

@cobie recalls an old tweet "that said something like 'you act too quickly when you are trying to grow capital, and you act too slowly when you are trying to protect capital' and it rly changed my life tbh".

One reading turns this into a lifecycle: move fast to grow, slowly to protect. Read literally, the tweet says the opposite: slow down when growing and act faster when protecting. [[Trading Philosophy]] follows the literal reading.

## Systems produce incentives

When a system looks broken, first ask whether it is producing exactly what its payoff matrix rewards (@incentivising). Find whose interests the structure serves, then change the game or play the one that exists ([[Decision-Making]]). Two bookmarks apply the lens: corporate innovation labs staffed by insulated senior people ship slide decks, not learning; and ordinary pay may not hold a hire who is already wealthy and can leave when work gets hard.

## Double opt-in introductions

Reid Hoffman's rule: ask both people privately before connecting them, give each enough context to decide, and connect only when both agree. It respects attention and raises the odds the match is useful.
