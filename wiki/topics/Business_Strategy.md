# Business Strategy

Business strategy in Erlin's notes is a set of borrowed frameworks rather than a single method. They cover how competition can be introduced on purpose, why distinctiveness costs continuous effort, what happens when price falls to zero, and how incentives decide outcomes. The page also holds a short venture-investor list and a set of product and founder leads. Most of the material comes from [[Business & Finance]], with links from [[Bookmarks - Business & Startups]] and [[X-Bookmarks]].

## Managed competition: the catfish effect

The catfish effect (鲶鱼效应) is a Chinese business-strategy metaphor: introduce a controlled competitor so that a stagnant system starts moving again. A [post](https://x.com/ruima/status/1937720853829157027) kept in [[Business & Finance]] and [[Culture & Observations]] lays it out _(@ruima)_. [[Catfish Effect]] has its own page.

- **Origin.** The story supposedly comes from Norwegian fishing. Sardines carried in tanks stopped moving, ran out of oxygen and died. A single live catfish in the tank kept them swimming, and more survived the journey. "The catfish made the environment slightly hostile, which weirdly made it more survivable."
- **Managed agitation, not disruption.** The post calls it "managed agitation" rather than disruption as a natural market event: "let's introduce a little stress on purpose, because stagnation is the bigger risk here." It says the idea spread through Chinese corporate training and state-aligned business commentary, and puts that down to culture: "more acceptance of discomfort as a developmental tool".
- **Case study: Tesla in China.** The government was frustrated with its domestic EV sector, where the subsidy programme had fed abuse and fraud and "a bunch of half-baked companies just trying to cash in on government money". It gave Tesla a rare set of approvals: full ownership of its China operations, fast-tracked factory deals, land and logistics support. The post's framing: "hey all of y'all Chinese companies, you guys need to be way faster cuz here comes the catfish. Swim!" It claims the pace of innovation has picked up since, in operations, design and user experience, while conceding "it wasn't all because of Tesla" and "I'm sure some will disagree with this example".
- **Difference from the West.** In the US, competition mostly arrives as disruptive innovation. In China, the post says, "competition can be curated, introduced, even welcomed -- not to destroy the domestic players, but to get them moving again." Curating competition this way takes trust and long-term thinking that quarterly capitalism tends to lack.
- **Limits.** "Of course, it doesn't always work. Sometimes the catfish is too strong, or the sardines were already too weak. And sometimes you get eaten, LOL."

The post ends on a personal question: "is that tank you're in the best environment for you? Are you swimming because you want to, or because you finally have to?"

## Distinctiveness as a moat

Jeff Bezos's last annual shareholder letter as Amazon's CEO, titled "Differentiation is Survival and the Universe Wants You to be Typical", is kept in full in [[Business & Finance]]. It opens with a passage from Richard Dawkins's *The Blind Watchmaker*: living bodies work constantly to stay out of equilibrium with their environment, and when the work stops they die and merge into their surroundings. Bezos applies this to companies, institutions and individual lives. See [[Jeff Bezos]].

- "The world wants you to be typical - in a thousand ways, it pulls at you. Don't let it happen."
- "You have to pay a price for your distinctiveness, and it's worth it."
- "Democracies are not normal. Tyranny is the historical norm."
- "The world will always try to make Amazon more typical - to bring us into equilibrium with our environment."

Read as strategy, distinctiveness is a running cost, not a one-off choice. Amazon's challenge is to stay distinctive against a thousand pulls toward the average. Most companies fail because the cost of standing apart exceeds its perceived value; the winners pay that cost.

## Pricing at zero

The note's claim: "You can make things cheap and it will fix a lot of problems that will arise by making things free. Weird things happen at 0.00." (from [[Business & Finance]])

- **Free healthcare.** Asked why Singapore does not provide free medical care, Ong Ye Kung answers: "The problem is that when healthcare is free, and it's all paid for already by taxes, people have no discipline. The demand just booms." He says a simple X-ray on the NHS takes nine months to a year, with seven million people on the waiting list: "The NHS is crumbling under the demand."
- **Free college.** San Francisco made its community college free, "And now apparently the slots get filled with scammers".

When price falls to zero:

- Demand becomes irrational and effectively unlimited.
- Systems collapse under the weight.
- Incentive structures break.
- Scarcity disappears, and abundance paradoxically becomes a liability.

Marginal cost structure drives behaviour. Price communicates scarcity; a price of zero communicates unlimited supply. The note follows the examples with a one-liner: "All profit comes from margins." See [[Economics]].

## Growing versus protecting capital

A line attributed to @cobie in [[Business & Finance]] recalls an old tweet "that said something like 'you act too quickly when you are trying to grow capital, and you act too slowly when you are trying to protect capital' and it rly changed my life tbh".

A lifecycle reading splits a business into two phases:

- **Growth phase:** act quickly, take risks. Speed over precision.
- **Protection phase:** act slowly, preserve capital. Security over opportunity.

On that reading, most entrepreneurs reverse these instinctively. Read literally, though, the quote names quick action while growing and slow action while protecting as the mistake, which points the other way: slow down when growing capital, act faster when protecting it. [[Capital Allocation Timing]] follows the literal reading.

## Consumer spending and gender

A [post](https://x.com/bubblebabyboi/status/1961989514571751434) in the note: "The economy is propped up by women, they spend money like nobody else. I've been around billionaires who watch their dollars closer than the average 20 year old in a big city." _(@bubblebabyboi)_

- Women control or influence up to 85% of US consumer spending, according to the figures the note gathers.
- Some sources put women's control of global spending at over $31.8 trillion.
- Women make most household purchasing decisions, especially for essentials like groceries, and strongly influence spending on homes and cars.

The implication drawn here: understanding female consumer psychology means understanding the structure of the economy. See [[Gender Dynamics]].

## Systems produce incentives

When a system appears broken, first ask whether it is producing exactly the outcome its payoff matrix rewards. More effort inside the same structure raises output without changing who benefits. Identify whose interests the structure serves, then either change the game or play the game that actually exists ([post](https://x.com/incentivising/status/2081434700711596312), from [[Thoughts & Observations]]) _(@incentivising)_. This connects business design to [[Decision-Making]].

Two business bookmarks apply the same lens. Corporate innovation labs staffed by insulated senior people tend to produce slide decks rather than shipped learning. Ordinary pay may not hold a hire who is already wealthy and can leave when work gets hard. Both are under Resources.

## Double opt-in introductions

Ask both people privately before connecting them, and give each enough context to decide. Make the connection only after both agree. This respects attention, avoids social pressure, and raises the chance that the match is useful ([post](https://x.com/reidhoffman/status/2079177965766635936), from [[Business & Finance]]) _(@reidhoffman)_. Agent-generated relationship suggestions should follow the same consent boundary; see [[AI Agents]].
