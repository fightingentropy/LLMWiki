# Health Evidence

Erlin's health bookmarks keep setting a vivid post, clip or before-and-after photo against the narrower question the underlying research can actually answer. This page turns that habit into a method: a ladder for weighing evidence, saved claims checked against the research, the association traps that recur, the claims still unresolved, and the corrections made to earlier notes. The full annotation for every bookmark is in [[Bookmarks - Health & Psychology]]. Nothing here is a treatment plan; medication, procedures and screening decisions belong with a clinician.

## Evidence ladder

1. A clip or personal report is a lead, not efficacy evidence.
2. An ex-vivo or animal result does not establish a clinical outcome in people.
3. A small pilot can establish feasibility while leaving safety, dose, and comparative effectiveness unresolved.
4. A randomized trial supports only its population, intervention, comparator, duration, and measured outcomes.
5. Product choice still depends on contraindications, formulation, dose, monitoring, and individual context.

Examples of each rung from the bookmarks (from [[Bookmarks - Health & Psychology]]):

- **Personal reports.** A [household-bleach sinus-rinse anecdote](https://x.com/EdaG889915/status/2052346899600998694). Chris Beiser's [intralymphatic immunotherapy report](https://x.com/ctbeiser/status/2085089503908413470), which he later scaled back to an estimated 65–70% reduction in allergy symptoms rather than a "permanent fix". A [post](https://x.com/minordissent/status/2094564810554052961) saying several peptides felt noticeable while most supplements felt like placebo, from an author who concedes peptides are overhyped; one person's experience, not comparative evidence of efficacy, safety, purity, dosing or legality.
- **Ex-vivo, in-vitro and animal work.** A [bioengineered chewing-gum study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41656354/) run on collected saliva and oral-rinse samples. A [2016 rat study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2016.07.019) that transferred pooled fecal microbiota from three severely depressed men (or three matched controls) into microbiota-depleted rats and reported anhedonia- and anxiety-like behaviour plus altered tryptophan metabolism; small preclinical evidence, not proof that human depression is transmissible. A [2020 in-vitro study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32517615/) in which follicular fluid from different women consistently attracted sperm from particular men differently, and the stronger response did not systematically favour the woman's partner; a possible gamete-mediated selection mechanism, not conscious choice or a clinical infertility test.
- **Pilots and single patients.** A 2013 evaluator-blinded [microneedling-plus-minoxidil pilot](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23960389/) in 100 men reported mean 12-week hair-count increases of 91.4 versus 22.2 in a fixed 1 cm² area; a later [meta-analysis of RCTs](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40056230/) supports better hair counts than minoxidil alone, but protocols and study quality vary. A [customised in-vivo base editor](https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMoa2504747) given to one infant with severe CPS1 deficiency let the patient tolerate more dietary protein over seven weeks without a serious treatment-related adverse event; an N-of-1 case with short follow-up. A [streaming brain-to-voice neuroprosthesis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-025-01905-6) synthesised speech in her pre-injury voice for one 47-year-old woman with severe paralysis; an invasive single-participant proof of concept.
- **Randomized trials.** In a 12-week [water-preloading trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2859815/) of 48 adults aged 55–75 with overweight or obesity, both groups ate a hypocaloric diet and one also drank 500 mL of water before each main meal. The water group lost about 2 kg more and had a 44% greater rate of weight loss. That does not support the [bookmarked post's](https://x.com/SmartNotHard1/status/2083606184594919582) claims of "no other dietary changes", ghrelin dilution, a 24–30% metabolism boost, faster liver fat metabolism, zero side effects, or equivalence to a meal-delivery programme. It is a modest adjunct, not a standalone protocol. The mobile-internet trial under Psychology and behaviour below is another case.
- **Product choice.** Tazarotene and tretinoin are both prescription retinoids, so titration, contraindications and pregnancy precautions decide between them, not a ranking. Red-light devices differ by indication, wavelength and dose. NHS vitamin D advice names a dose and a season, not a stack.

## Saved claims checked against the research

Each of these pairs a confident post with what the cited research supports. (from [[Bookmarks - Health & Psychology]])

- **Tazarotene versus tretinoin.** The [post](https://x.com/Hoostethics/status/2093649112352821464) calls tazarotene categorically stronger, using an uncontrolled before-and-after image of a routine that also included azelaic acid and moisturisers, and suggests skipping straight to it. Randomized photoageing trials ([24-week comparison](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15203997/); [multicentre concentration trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11735710/)) found that tazarotene may outperform tretinoin on selected photoageing measures in some concentrations and formulations, with more early irritation. This is not a universal ranking across acne, pigmentation, wrinkles, every concentration, or every patient.
- **Red-light therapy.** The [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/cosmetic/safety/red-light-therapy) describes evidence for some skin-ageing concerns and hereditary hair loss, with results varying by device, treatment and person. That is indication- and device-specific evidence, not proof of the [post's](https://x.com/dantefofante/status/2091994204365009297) universal benefits for mood, recovery, energy and health from an unspecified Amazon device. Photosensitising medication, skin sensitivity and eye protection matter.
- **Bleach in a sinus rinse.** An anecdote is not a protocol. The author [reports feeling better](https://x.com/EdaG889915/status/2052346899600998694) after adding diluted household sodium hypochlorite to a sinus rinse and posted a [follow-up mixing instruction](https://x.com/EdaG889915/status/2052350686268887512). A [small randomized study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27853946/) of device-generated low-concentration hypochlorous acid in selected chronic-rhinosinusitis patients does not validate improvised sodium-hypochlorite mixtures; the two are not interchangeable. [FDA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/rinsing-your-sinuses-neti-pots-safe) describes saline made with distilled, sterile or previously boiled water, and CDC bleach instructions concern disinfecting otherwise unsafe water, not adding bleach as a therapeutic ingredient. Do not copy the mixture; persistent symptoms and any nonstandard rinse go to an ENT clinician.
- **Smoking and long COVID.** A [clip](https://x.com/Bitcoin_Teddy/status/2086392291862995011) claims smokers get only mild COVID, cannot develop long COVID, that nicotine blocks spike protein, and that nicotine patches are harmless. Smoking does not prevent long COVID: [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/long-covid/about/index.html) says anyone infected can develop it, and [CDC's risk evidence](https://www.cdc.gov/covid/hcp/clinical-care/underlying-conditions.html) associates smoking with severe COVID outcomes. Tobacco damages nearly every organ and nicotine is addictive ([WHO tobacco fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/tobacco)). Nicotine-replacement products have defined cessation uses and contraindications; the clip does not make patches a long-COVID treatment.
- **Trading alcohol harm for cigarette harm.** A [post](https://x.com/algekalipso/status/2086859052181786862) uses a convex-versus-concave marginal-risk analogy to propose that some people should "drink less, smoke more." A population-risk analogy does not justify substituting cigarettes for alcohol: population risk curves do not establish a safe personal substitution, tobacco is harmful at every level, alcohol is also carcinogenic and not risk-free ([WHO alcohol fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/alcohol)), and using one addictive exposure to regulate another can compound harm rather than optimise it.
- **Intralymphatic immunotherapy (ILIT).** Controlled evidence is mixed and systemic reactions are reported, so an anecdotal improvement is a specialist discussion lead, not a cure. Beiser [reports](https://x.com/ctbeiser/status/2085089503908413470) lasting relief for likely dust-mite allergy, nighttime congestion and poor sleep; another participant in the thread reports no benefit or possible worsening. A [randomized double-blind trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34088322/) using dust-mite, cat and dog extracts found little overall clinical advantage over placebo and recorded pain plus moderate-to-severe systemic reactions. A tiny [11-person pilot](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28293934/) reported symptom improvement but included two cases of anaphylaxis.
- **Bioengineered chewing gum.** The [ex-vivo study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41656354/) exposed collected samples to bean lectin and antimicrobial-peptide gum preparations and reported aggregation of up to 93% of detected HPV and reductions in selected bacteria. Gum that aggregates HPV or selected bacteria ex vivo does not show that ordinary chewing clears infection or prevents cancer; in-vivo safety, dosing, efficacy and replication are still needed. ([original bookmark](https://x.com/Polymarket/status/2088726104424976834))
- **Prostate cancer and PSA screening.** UK prostate-cancer incidence is not, by itself, an argument for universal PSA screening; overdiagnosis and downstream harm remain part of the decision. Macmillan estimates, as [reported by Sky News](https://news.sky.com/story/one-man-diagnosed-with-prostate-cancer-in-the-uk-every-eight-minutes-13575557), put UK diagnoses above 68,000 a year, around one every eight minutes, with roughly 600,000 men living with the disease; population ageing and increased detection affect the trend. Early disease can be symptomless, but the [UK National Screening Committee](https://view-health-screening-recommendations.service.gov.uk/prostate-cancer/) does not recommend population-wide PSA screening and currently recommends targeted screening for a defined high-risk BRCA2 group. ([original bookmark](https://x.com/bertdernernert/status/2091055197405557210))

## Finding the narrower claim

Several bookmarks overreach yet contain a narrower point the research does support. Keep the narrow point; drop the rest. (from [[Bookmarks - Health & Psychology]])

- **Testosterone and depressive symptoms.** The [post](https://x.com/wanted4mogging/status/2094567596490162625) argues that prescribing antidepressants without testosterone screening proves systemic failure. The narrower point: low mood, energy, libido, concentration and sexual symptoms can overlap with hypogonadism and may justify clinical evaluation. Per the [Endocrine Society guideline](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/testosterone-therapy), diagnosis needs compatible symptoms plus consistently low, accurately measured morning testosterone, not universal screening or the assumption that testosterone is the cause.
- **H. pylori and levothyroxine.** A [2011 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21435090/) found that treating H. pylori in hypothyroid patients not responding to high-dose levothyroxine substantially lowered TSH, plausibly by improving absorption; 21% then developed iatrogenic thyrotoxicosis. It does not validate the [post's](https://x.com/FarvingCo/status/2093783673623740918) broad symptom list, belly-fat mechanism, low-T3 certainty, or self-directed supplement "kill stack". H. pylori needs proper testing and clinician-directed eradication therapy.
- **Demodex mites and rosacea.** An [anecdotal post](https://x.com/SMB_Attorney/status/2093856369203888444) says prescription ivermectin transformed persistent skin problems. The [National Rosacea Society](https://www.rosacea.org/rosacea-review/2025/spring/topical-ivermectin-may-improve-rosacea-indirectly) supports the narrow version: Demodex density can be elevated in rosacea and topical ivermectin is an established treatment for papulopustular rosacea. Mites also live on healthy skin and are not a universal explanation for acne, redness or irritation.
- **Sex differences in mental rotation.** The [post](https://x.com/antarte_/status/2093756333010952414) extrapolates from a water-bottle rotation question to broad claims about women, systemizing and IQ-test construction. A [2026 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41566416/) reports an average male advantage on mental-rotation tests, with substantial heterogeneity. It does not substantiate the post's "40%" college-women statistic, its general-intelligence conclusion, or the claim that psychometricians deliberately removed systemizing to equalise scores.
- **Depression, memory and practised skills.** [NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/depression) lists difficulty concentrating, remembering or making decisions as a recognised symptom of depression, and severe cases can involve prominent memory and thinking problems. That supports one [author's lived experience](https://x.com/Laiiyylaa/status/2085925048335126831), a later [anhedonia-to-skill-loss model](https://x.com/thehowlchemist/status/2094249098312384915) (reduced pleasure and practice can plausibly erode fluency), and a [general observation](https://x.com/DEUILSTEARS444/status/2096602733654552989) that depression and anxiety can come with substantial memory problems. None establishes inevitable permanent "brain damage", neurotoxicity or irreversible pruning. Persistent cognitive symptoms deserve clinical assessment, not self-diagnosis from a post.

## Association is not causation

- **Push-ups and heart disease.** The Body note says: "Men who can do 40 push-ups or more have a 96 percent reduced risk of heart disease compared with men who could do less than 10 push-ups." The [2019 JAMA Network Open study](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2724778) behind it followed 1,104 active male firefighters; greater baseline push-up capacity was associated with fewer cardiovascular events. The 96% figure compares groups in an observational cohort. It is not a reduction caused by doing forty push-ups and not a universal cardiac screening test. (from [[Health Evidence Review - September 2026]] and [[Body]])
- **Repetitive negative thinking and Alzheimer's biomarkers.** [@dhh's humorous reaction](https://x.com/dhh/status/2101644926094373128) quotes a claim from @NTFabiano that repetitive negative thinking is associated with Alzheimer's-related protein accumulation. Until the underlying study, population, measures and effect size are found, it is an association claim. It does not establish that worrying causes dementia or that optimism prevents it.
- **Cannabis and DNA methylation.** A [cross-sectional CanCOLD subset](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-025-03634-9) of 93 older adults, including 10 current and 32 former cannabis smokers, found thousands of blood-DNA methylation associations versus never-smokers, overlapping ageing- and cancer-related pathways. It cannot establish causation, persistence within individuals, downstream gene expression or clinical cancer risk; the authors note self-report, tobacco confounding, small groups and no repeated measures.
- **Body-odour disgust and attitudes to outsiders.** A [preregistered study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10090875/) of 6,836 participants in nine countries found greater self-reported sensitivity to body odours was associated with more negative attitudes toward fictitious refugee groups, partly through perceived differences in hygiene and food-preparation norms. A survey association; it does not show that disgust determines anyone's politics, and the [bookmark's crude punchline](https://x.com/Basedgoymemes/status/2093361464119566353) does not follow from it.
- **Mind-wandering and happiness.** Killingsworth and Gilbert's [2010 experience-sampling study](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1192439) found people reported lower momentary happiness while their minds wandered, even toward neutral or pleasant topics. It is observational self-report about attention and mood. It is not evidence that consuming instead of creating causes clinical depression, or that finding a "creative quest" is a diagnosis or a cure. ([original bookmark](https://x.com/NTFabiano/status/2086444739805532641))

## Psychology and behaviour

The paternal-investment bookmark is a useful interpretation check: distinguish perceived resemblance from objective relatedness, reported involvement from observed behaviour, and trait scores from diagnoses. A statistical interaction does not reveal a parent's motive. The [2020 study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2020.109879) behind the [post](https://x.com/houseofhereaftr/status/2103150600753950923) surveyed 158 Romanian father–teen pairs and linked perceived resemblance to reported paternal investment, with a stronger facial-resemblance association at higher psychopathy scores; personality similarity also contributed. Survey associations do not establish causation, change over time, clinical diagnoses, or a motive of self-love. The [quoted post](https://x.com/Psikobilim_/status/2102824105024332196) and the [university abstract](https://www.research.unipd.it/handle/11577/3359563) carry the details. (from [[Bookmarks - Health & Psychology]])

Limerence is a descriptive concept, not an official diagnosis. ADHD-related hyperfocus may be one vulnerability, but reciprocal knowledge of a real person should be separated from a checking-and-rumination loop built around an imagined relationship. [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/limerence) discusses insecure attachment and a possible hyperfocus vulnerability; the [original post](https://x.com/apaji_x/status/2091560421555614093) overstates limerence as a severe dopamine addiction. Persistent distress or impairment is a reason to seek professional support. See [[ADHD]] and [[Mental Health]].

A [randomized mobile-internet blocking study](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/4/2/pgaf017/8016017), a preregistered month-long trial, had participants block smartphone internet for two weeks while keeping calls, texts, and internet access on other devices. Intention-to-treat analyses found improvements in subjective well-being, mental health and objectively measured sustained attention, but compliance was difficult: only 119 of 467 committed participants met the preregistered compliance threshold. The "ten years younger" comparison is an effect-size analogy. The result supports testing attention boundaries, not claims of literal rejuvenation. ([original bookmark](https://x.com/NTFabiano/status/2084270984022905286); [further discussion](https://x.com/sentientist/status/2054591492162040312))

## Contradictions and unresolved claims

- **ADHD and the body clock.** Erlin's medical note saves the line "We recently reconceptualized ADHD as a circadian rhythm disorder." beside a [post by Nicholas Fabiano](https://x.com/NTFabiano/status/2020162108919357483). The source is a [perspective about a subgroup](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1697900/full), summarised in [Brandon Luu's thread](https://x.com/BrandonLuuMD/status/2102338491942347175); see the corrected [[ADHD]] and [[Circadian Rhythm]] pages.
- **Stimulant medication.** A [negative account of prescribed Vyvanse](https://x.com/LawrenceElliots/status/2102046696142238206) and a [positive account of Adderall](https://x.com/Gena_I_Gorlin/status/2101802695325528433) are kept together as individual experiences, not a universal verdict on medication. Medication concerns belong with the prescriber.
- **Quick fixes.** The [three-day depression and ADHD cure](https://x.com/oxidativestate/status/2097515710172025053), the [antihistamine comparison for "ADHD brains"](https://x.com/be_like_legend1/status/2096567715519434845) and the [histamine-and-sleep thread](https://x.com/ArsAlendi/status/2096617680815980866) remain unverified claims. A product or vendor reference, such as [Peptides Lab UK](https://peptideslabuk.com/), is not clinical evidence.
- **Worry and dementia.** Repetitive negative thinking and Alzheimer's biomarkers is an association claim pending the underlying study (see above). It does not establish that worrying causes dementia.
- **Claims without methods.** [Imagined muscle contractions building strength](https://x.com/JIMMYEDGAR/status/2101074736600011033), [wearable-guided back-pain physical therapy](https://x.com/thefountaindead/status/2097089544357327268), and [resisting cravings at peak intensity for more "rewiring"](https://x.com/alexeixbt/status/2100716907108151736) need specific methods and outcomes before practical conclusions. The wearable demo does not validate its model or show the suggested exercises are safe; exposure and addiction work can need professional support.
- **Purpose as a depression cure.** The bookmark annotation on a [motivational post](https://x.com/minordissent/status/2094327547672805434) rejects purpose as a universal depression cure, contradicting earlier absolute wording in [[Mental Health]]. Saving the motivational post does not settle that clinical question; depression has heterogeneous biological, psychological and social contributors.
- **Remedies saved in Erlin's notes.** The notes supply no controlled clinical evidence for these, so they remain attributed claims awaiting their own review. That is a judgement on the notes' support, not proof that every possible effect is absent. (from [[Health Evidence Review - September 2026]], [[Body]] and [[Supplements]])
  - Blueberries: "Near-instant cure for brain fog: Eat 500g+ of blueberries in one sitting" ([source post](https://x.com/DejaRu22/status/1884529318564708759)).
  - Magnesium: "Mg can protect against hearing loss, solid data on reducing migraines & it can improve sleep."
  - Tongkat ali: "Pomegranate juice to offset oestrogen block from tongkat Ali".
  - Cold showers: five-minute cold showers taken when anxious, which "almost feels like taking a pill or something." The comparison to medication is unsupported.

## Corrections to earlier notes

**Correction, 22 September 2026:** primary sources contradict or narrow the fluoride, dopamine, push-up, vitamin D and peptide-preparation claims in [[Medical]], [[Body]], [[Fitness]], [[Supplements]] and [[Peptides]]. [[Health Evidence Review - September 2026]] sets out the sources, and each correction sits at the claim it corrects.

- **Fluoride toothpaste.** The medical note says "Fluoride competes with iodine at thyroid receptor sites." and recommends hydroxyapatite toothpaste instead. [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/healthy-teeth-and-gums/how-to-keep-your-teeth-clean/) recommends fluoride toothpaste twice daily, at least 1,350 ppm fluoride for adults. The note supplies no clinical evidence for the thyroid theory in normal toothpaste use.
- **Dopamine and sunlight.** The medical note says of tyrosine hydroxylase, "Sunlight is needed for this step." The reaction uses tyrosine, oxygen and tetrahydrobiopterin; sunlight is not a reagent ([PubChem catecholamine pathway](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pathway/BioCyc%3AHUMAN_PWY66-301); [original enzyme-kinetics study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6117320/)). Light's effect on circadian timing is a separate question.
- **Push-ups.** The 96% figure is an observational group comparison (see above). For a baseline, [NHS adult activity guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/physical-activity-guidelines-for-adults-aged-19-to-64/) for ages 19–64 is 150 minutes of moderate or 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week, with strength work on at least two days, fitted to health and ability.
- **Vitamin D.** [NHS guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/) says adults should consider 10 micrograms daily in autumn and winter, and some groups need it year-round. That is a specific public-health recommendation, not support for the note's whole supplement stack.
- **Retatrutide.** The [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-alerts-and-statements/fdas-concerns-unapproved-glp-1-drugs-used-weight-loss) states retatrutide is not a component of an FDA-approved drug and has not been found safe and effective for any condition. That is a US regulatory statement, not a finding about every country. Research-vendor products and a saved injection guide do not establish clinical suitability, identity, purity or stability. See [[Retatrutide]].
- **Bacteriostatic water and reconstitution.** The [DailyMed label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/fda/fdaDrugXsl.cfm?setid=ccadcf46-6a6f-436b-9bbc-17e2983a335f&type=display) describes 0.9% benzyl alcohol and directs use according to the drug manufacturer's instructions; compatibility varies. A preservative does not validate a universal 28-day shelf life for every reconstituted drug, and the note's shaking, freezing and room-temperature potency-loss percentages have no supporting stability study. See [[Bacteriostatic Water]] and [[Peptide Reconstitution]].
