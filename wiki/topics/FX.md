# FX

Foreign exchange is where macro regimes often show up first: currencies tend to move before equities and bonds. The daily brief asks for "USD broad, key crosses, EM stress signals" ([[Markets - Brief]]). The dollar sits at the centre.

## The dollar and DXY

The US Dollar Index (DXY) measures the dollar against a fixed basket of six currencies, based at 100 in March 1973.

| Currency | Weight |
| --- | --- |
| Euro | 57.6% |
| Japanese yen | 13.6% |
| British pound | 11.9% |
| Canadian dollar | 9.1% |
| Swedish krona | 4.2% |
| Swiss franc | 3.6% |

- With the euro over half the basket, DXY moves largely as the inverse of EUR/USD. China and Mexico are not in it.
- It usually trades between 90 and 110. The extremes were about 165 (February 1985) and about 71 (March 2008).
- 100 is the anchor. Above 105 is extreme strength, rarely sustained; below 95 is extreme weakness. Extremes tend to revert.
- A strong dollar usually means risk-off, high US rates or global stress; a weak dollar means risk-on and easier money.

## What the dollar moves

- **Commodities:** priced in dollars, so a weak dollar lifts them ([[Commodities]]).
- **Gold and silver:** "rates low or dollar weaker = gold more attractive" (from [[Markets - Handbook]]).
- **Emerging markets:** a weak dollar means EM strength; a strong dollar means EM stress.
- **US earnings:** a strong dollar shrinks foreign earnings once translated into dollars.
- **Trade:** a strong dollar makes US exports dearer and imports cheaper, so it favours importers; a weak dollar favours exporters. Anyone owing dollars but earning another currency suffers when the dollar rises.

Correction (15 October 2026): an earlier version had the exporter and importer effects, and the yen carry unwind, the wrong way round.

## Key crosses and EM stress

| Cross | What moves it |
| --- | --- |
| EUR/USD | ECB versus Fed rates; Eurozone growth |
| GBP/USD | BoE versus Fed; UK growth |
| USD/JPY | The yen is a safe haven and the carry-trade barometer; moves can be sharp |

EM currencies weaken first in risk-off as capital flees, and their weakness tends to lead EM equities. A broad EM currency breakdown signals systemic stress.

## Rates drive currencies

- Currencies price rate expectations quickly, often ahead of the rates themselves.
- When US rates or real rates rise above the rest of the world, the dollar rallies; when they fall below, it weakens ([[Rates and Credit]]).

## The yen carry trade

A carry trade borrows a low-rate currency, classically the yen, to buy a high-rate one. In stress it reverses: the yen strengthens and leveraged positions are forced out. A rallying yen signals stress; a weak yen signals complacency.

@TheOtherParker_ traced one possible unwind in February 2026. After IBIT's record $10.7B volume day, he guessed that Hong Kong funds running levered IBIT options on yen-funded capital were hurt on 10 October, squeezed as "the JPY carry trade has been unwinding at an increasingly rapid pace", moved into silver, and were finished off by a last leg down in BTC. His own caveat: "I have no hard evidence here, just some hunches and bread crumbs".

## Is a currency cheap or hated?

[[goodalexander]]'s contrarian checklist has tests written for currencies:

- **Valuation:** "For currencies can consider PPP (it's cheap to buy stuff in Japan and expensive to buy stuff in Switzerland)".
- **Borrow cost:** "For fx if something has a high yield and a low credit default risk this is a reasonable proxy" for people not wanting to bet against it.
- **Macro consensus:** what forecasts expect for balance-sheet expansion, rate cuts, employment and inflation.

He calls such data "a much more reliable guide to positioning than what people say publicly" ([[Positioning and Sentiment]]).

## Currency as policy

A long passage in [[Crypto Takes]], from a post by @SuitablePolitic, reads Bessent's tariffs as a plan for the dollar:

1. Bessent "was hired to strengthen the dollar by doing the opposite of all the warning signs he saw in the British Pound", where inflated housing and Treasury yields "pointed to the big short that the Soros fund pulled off".
2. Pushing on home prices and bond yields strengthens "the leading indicators of a currency": "This is what I mean by "white hat hacking" the dollar."
3. "Wall Street was on a sugar high", and tariff volatility "was a way of creating controlled detonation" that forced deleveraging.
4. Boxing in China's exports could collapse the yuan and "cause a global flight into US Treasury bonds", lowering rates "for when they go to refinance the $9T of expiring debt."
