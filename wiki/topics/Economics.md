# Economics

Economics in this wiki is mostly price theory and incentives: what happens when things become free, who does the spending, how labels differ from outcomes, and how markets get built for assets that lack one. Most of the economic content sits inside [[Markets]], [[Business Strategy]] and [[Political Commentary]]; this page collects the cross-cutting observations from [[Bizz_and_Finance|Business & Finance]], [[Culture|Culture & Observations]] and [[Politics]]. For macro and trading economics see [[Macro Trading]], [[Rates]] and [[Credit]]. The common thread: follow incentives and outcomes, not rhetoric.

## Weird things happen at zero

> "You can make things cheap and it will fix a lot of problems that will arise by making things free. Weird things happen at 0.00."

When the price falls to zero, the scarcity signal disappears, demand has no brake, and the system breaks. The note gives two cases (from [[Bizz_and_Finance|Business & Finance]]):

- **Free healthcare**: asked why Singapore does not provide free medical care, Ong Ye Kung answers: "The problem is that when healthcare is free, and it's all paid for already by taxes, people have no discipline. The demand just booms." He cites a wait of nine months to a year for a simple X-ray on the NHS and seven million people on the waiting list.
- **Free college**: San Francisco made its community college free, "And now apparently the slots get filled with scammers".

## Who does the spending

> "The economy is propped up by women, they spend money like nobody else. I've been around billionaires who watch their dollars closer than the average 20 year old in a big city."

That line links to a [post by @bubblebabyboi](https://x.com/bubblebabyboi/status/1961989514571751434). The note backs it with figures it attributes to unnamed sources (from [[Bizz_and_Finance|Business & Finance]]):

- Women control or influence up to 85% of US consumer spending.
- Some sources put women's global spending control at over $31.8 trillion.
- Women make the majority of household purchasing decisions, especially for essentials like groceries, and significantly influence spending on new homes and cars.

See [[Gender Dynamics]] for the wider theme.

## Labels versus outcomes

A note in [[Politics]] argues that the UK has a far flatter income distribution than the Soviet Union: wage compression and taxes leave a net income difference of only about 3x between minimum wage and a top 5% salary.

| Measure | Figure |
| --- | --- |
| UK take-home pay, full-time (40 hours) minimum wage | £22,555 |
| UK take-home pay at a £100k salary | £68,558 |
| UK net income ratio | 3.04:1 |
| USSR, same comparison | never fell below 5:1 |

The note adds that it is "even worse in reality because the person earning £100k in the UK often has student loans", and concludes:

> "Britain is nominally capitalist but functionally communist. China is nominally communist but functionally capitalist. Funny how that works."

These are the note's figures, not checked against official data. The analysis is developed in [[Political Commentary]].

## Managed competition: the catfish effect

China's [[Catfish Effect]] is a policy tool: introduce a curated outside competitor to break domestic stagnation. The story supposedly comes from a Norwegian fishing practice: one live catfish in a tank of transported sardines keeps them moving, so more survive the journey. The [thread by @ruima](https://x.com/ruima/status/1937720853829157027) calls it "more like managed agitation" rather than disruption as a natural market phenomenon (from [[Bizz_and_Finance|Business & Finance]] and [[Culture|Culture & Observations]]).

- **The Tesla case**: frustrated with a domestic EV sector full of subsidy abuse and fraud, the Chinese government gave Tesla rare approvals: full ownership of its China operations, fast-tracked factory deals, land and logistics support. The thread says the pace of innovation picked up afterwards, though not all because of Tesla.
- **The contrast**: Western business theory tends towards disruptive innovation; in China, competition "can be curated, introduced, even welcomed -- not to destroy the domestic players, but to get them moving again."
- **The limit**: "Sometimes the catfish is too strong, or the sardines were already too weak."

## Markets for illiquid assets

Two ideas in [[Bizz_and_Finance|Business & Finance]] are about building market structure where none exists:

- **Perpetual futures**: the note calls perps "the best pmf in all of crypto", linking a [post by @sershokunin](https://x.com/sershokunin/status/1945857878771818753), and adds that Robert Shiller proposed perpetual futures in 1992. Shiller's aim was derivatives markets for illiquid, infrequently priced assets such as single-family homes, and for untraded indices and income flows such as labour costs or the consumer price index.
- **Tokenization**: a thread of replies in the note lists real estate, IP and start-up shares as candidates. The core claim: "The assets that benefit the most from tokenization will be those that don't currently have a robust market structure." The case for it is liquidity in illiquid markets, 24/7 global trading instead of stock market hours (see [[Exchange Hours]]), and borrowing against tokenized assets as collateral. One reply expects such tokens to trade at massive premiums but says they at least make hard-to-reach assets investable for normal people. The pushback in the same thread: "You don't own that. Your broker owns that on your behalf".
