# Markets

This page holds the general market ideas in Erlin's finance note ([[Business & Finance]]): how price relates to news, when the US market is busy or quiet, how reflexive pumps form, how to split an equity valuation, how bear markets unfold, and why perpetual futures and tokenization matter for market structure. It also carries two economics takes the note files beside them: women's share of consumer spending, and what happens when a price reaches zero. Most lines are pasted takes from traders and threads, not tested rules. For the full map of trading pages, start at [[Markets Wiki Index]].

## Price moves before the news

Price action comes first and the explanation follows. What looks like a market predicting the news is usually a move that already happened, with the story arriving later.

> Markets are typically 6-8 months ahead of the news. Price moves first, and the "why" follows.
> Stocks peak when the best has already been discounted, with fundamentals often deteriorating later as expectations fail to match reality.
> "Stage analysis" helps in riding trends.

(from [[Business & Finance]])

Whether a headline is true matters less than how the market takes it. GCR ([[GiganticRebirth]]), 21 May 2021:

> When news impacts the price, market participants often fixate on whether it's true or not true.
>
> More often than not, the actual veracity of the headline is immaterial.
>
> It's how the market reacts to the news, and for how long, that turns out to be a lot more informative.

The read: watch the reaction and its duration, not the fact-check. The note's joke version of the same idea is a WallStreetBets shitpost: "Don't even ask the question. The answer is yes, it's priced in." Beside it sits a one-liner: "Stability in finance is nothing but an [illusion](https://twitter.com/resonancethis/status/1560571813125427201)" _(@resonancethis)_.

## The US trading calendar

The note pastes a longer passage on US market seasonality (from [[Business & Finance]]). "For the U.S. markets the year really begins in September. It's the end of summer, the kids are back in school, and traders are back at their desks."

| Period | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Last week of August | Exceptionally low volume, often stretching past Labor Day to the September FOMC meeting |
| September | The year really begins; traders return from summer |
| FOMC meeting days | One of eight scheduled meetings a year; "sure to see wild market gyrations, especially just after the committee releases its statement" |
| September to Christmas | Activity and volume stay robust |
| Day after Thanksgiving | A brief lull |
| Christmas to New Year | Relatively low activity |
| January 1 to May | Volume picks back up and stays seasonally strong |
| May to summer | "Sell in May and Go Away" as the lazy days of summer approach |
| Week after July 4 | The year's nadir: desks "abandoned for lake cabins, golf courses, or seaside resorts" |

Why it matters: low volume "can often lead to unexpected market movements that don't follow typical patterns." The passage breaks off mid-image: "Just as a placid lake surface suddenly pierced by a". For session times by exchange, see [[Exchange Hours]].

Correction, 15 October 2026: this page used to say activity is strong from January to October and that most annual moves happen from late August to December. The source says volume is strong from January until "Sell in May", that late August to the September FOMC meeting is a low-volume stretch, and nothing about where most of the year's moves happen.

## Reflexive pumps

Reflexive pumps don't start with buyers. They start with no one left willing to sell. The note tags @diablofutur alongside this:

> Reflexive pumps don't begin with a wave of buyers, they start with no one left willing to sell. A stubborn holder base that refuses to let go triggers a synthetic supply shock. Aggro-rage shorts pile in. A disbelief rally erupts, fuelled by sideline capital waiting on a nuke that never shows. FOMO kicks in, chasing price into thin air, with zero marginal sellers and infinite shorts left to cover. Parabola and attention games begin.

The sequence:

1. A stubborn holder base refuses to let go.
2. That creates a synthetic supply shock.
3. Aggressive shorts pile in.
4. A disbelief rally erupts; sideline capital waits for a dip that never comes.
5. FOMO kicks in and price chases into thin air.
6. Zero marginal sellers plus shorts that must cover produce parabolic moves.
7. Attention games take over.

A related take from [[Rewkang]] on reflexivity at all-time highs: "There is an element of reflexivity around ATH corrections. People sell because they see it's always rejected and then more people follow when it actually [happens](https://twitter.com/Rewkang/status/1765075321181929840). Short term acute flows ($10B+ shizo day traders) overwhelm passive flows ($500m+ ETF buying). Eventually, passive flows win out." See [[Crypto Trading]] and [[Market Cycles]].

## How bear markets unfold

Robert Rhea (1935), "Three Bear Phases", via @WifeyAlpha:

> Bear markets seem to be divided into three phases:
>
> the first being the abandonment of hopes upon which the uprush of the preceding bull market was predicated;
>
> the second being the reflection of the decreased earning power and reduction of dividends;
>
> the third representing distress liquidation of securities which must be sold to meet living expenses.
>
> Each of these phases seems to be divided by a secondary reaction which is often erroneously assumed to be the beginning of a bull market.

1. **Abandonment of hopes.** The story behind the bull market crumbles.
2. **Reduced earning power.** Earnings and dividends fall.
3. **Distress liquidation.** Holders must sell securities to cover living expenses.

The note adds that a secondary reaction is a bear-market rally, which is why each one gets mistaken for a new bull. See [[Market Cycles]].

## Perpetual futures

"[Perps](https://x.com/sershokunin/status/1945857878771818753) the best pmf in all of crypto…" _(@sershokunin)_

The idea is older than crypto. In 1992 Robert Shiller proposed perpetual futures, together with a way to build asset-price indices by hedonic regression. The aim was derivatives markets for illiquid, infrequently priced assets such as single-family homes, and for untraded indices and income flows such as labour costs or the consumer price index. In short: a market for things that are hard to price and have no traditional market. See [[Crypto Trading]] and [[Funding Rates]].

## Tokenization and market structure

Today, public and private securities sit in siloed databases run by banks, broker-dealers and law firms, or idle on cap-table software and spreadsheets. A thread in the note calls the result "value extraction, mistake, and inefficiency across all stages of an asset's lifecycle", including costly trading fees.

What tokenization offers, per the thread:

- Trading 24/7/365 in a global market, instead of US stock-market hours.
- Collateral value: borrowing against assets and posting them as collateral in other smart contracts.
- Lending and borrowing through non-custodial protocols.
- A broader pool of buyers, including onchain users, organisations and protocol DAOs.
- Sending assets anywhere, instantly.

The most benefit goes to assets without a robust market today: real estate, IP and start-up shares. "I would look beyond the most established, already liquid ETFs."

The thread argues both ways. One reply points out that with a broker "You don't own that. Your broker owns that on your behalf and you hold an account at your brokerage". A sceptic answers: "No one cares. We WANT to outsource all this bs, esp for no fee and it's regulated by SEC so we sleep at noight". The comeback: "Until you get debanked or account frozen due to your political speech online. Think Canadian truckers". Another reply makes the same ownership point: your investing app "will own it in T+1 days", so try borrowing against it on an open market or sending it to a friend in Yemen. The full exchange is on [[Business & Finance]].

## Economics in the same note

### Women and consumer spending

"The [economy](https://x.com/bubblebabyboi/status/1961989514571751434) is propped up by women, they spend money like nobody else. I've been around billionaires who watch their dollars closer than the average 20 year old in a big city." _(@bubblebabyboi)_

- Women control or influence up to 85% of US consumer spending.
- Some sources put women's global spending control at over $31.8 trillion.
- Women make most household purchasing decisions, especially for groceries, and strongly influence spending on new homes and cars.

The note gives no specific source for the figures. See [[Economics]].

### Weird things happen at $0.00

> You can make things cheap and it will fix a lot of problems that will arise by making things free. Weird things happen at 0.00.

When the price of a service drops to zero, demand outruns any rational limit, the system buckles under the load, and the incentives that kept it working break.

- **The NHS.** Singapore's Ong Ye Kung, asked why Singapore does not offer free medical care: "when healthcare is free, and it's all paid for already by taxes, people have no discipline. The demand just booms." His example: a simple X-ray on the NHS takes nine months to a year, with seven million people on the waiting list, and "The NHS is crumbling under the demand."
- **Free college.** San Francisco made its community college free, "And now apparently the slots get filled with scammers".
