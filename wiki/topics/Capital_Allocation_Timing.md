# Capital Allocation Timing

Most capital-allocation errors come from one asymmetry in speed: people act too quickly when trying to grow capital and too slowly when trying to protect it. The frame treats panic selling and hesitant buying as two faces of the same timing error rather than separate failure modes. It starts as a one-line heuristic in [[Bizz_and_Finance|Business & Finance]] and recurs in [[Trading_Psychology]], [[Behavioral_Finance]] and [[Trading Philosophy]]. This page collects what the notes add around it: cash as an option, seasonal timing, and surviving a strategy's normal losses.

## The asymmetry

> "you act too quickly when you are trying to grow capital, and you act too slowly when you are trying to protect capital"

- In [[Bizz_and_Finance|Business & Finance]] the line comes as a half-remembered tweet ("Idk who said it but I remember a long time ago a tweet that said something like…"), followed by "and it rly changed my life tbh", with the handle @cobie beside it.
- [[CT|Crypto Takes]] keeps the same line under "Someone once told Cobie". See [[cobie]].
- Read this way, a single inversion explains most losses: eagerness to add risk while growing, reluctance to cut it while protecting.
- A related warning on dwelling on drawdowns: "If you spend your time obsessing over old losses you have a high probability of generating new losses." Kris @KrisAbdelmessih, quoted in [[Bizz_and_Finance|Business & Finance]].

## Growth phase and protection phase

- [[Business_Strategy]] recasts the idea as a business lifecycle: the growth phase wants speed over precision, the protection phase wants security over opportunity, and most entrepreneurs reverse these instinctively. [[Business_Philosophy]] repeats this version.
- Contradiction: read literally, the tweet names quick action while growing and slow action while protecting as the mistake. That points the other way: slow down when growing capital, act faster when protecting it. This page follows the literal reading.

## Cash as an option

- A reply saved in [[Bizz_and_Finance|Business & Finance]]: "Cash = option / Option value is maximized by vol. / Inflation is option premium." The same idea appears in [[Trading_Psychology]] and [[Markets]].
- Holding cash through deep drawdowns preserves the ability to deploy. A passage in the note, linked to a [post by @Rewkang](https://twitter.com/Rewkang/status/1765075321181929840), describes reflexivity around all-time-high corrections: people sell because highs have always been rejected, and more follow when the rejection happens. Short-term flows overwhelm passive ETF buying until passive flows win out, "But there is some appeal of the optionality value of some cash to bid 40%+ wicks on alts." See [[Rewkang]].
- A line linked to a [post by @WClementeIII](https://twitter.com/WClementeIII/status/1765044203682136559) argues that independent thinking and exposure to extreme volatility will make crypto investors and traders some of the best-equipped capital allocators for generations.

## Seasonality

Capital-timing errors compound with market seasonality: low-volume summer periods and September FOMC gyrations punish ill-timed deployment ([[Trading_Psychology]]). The seasonal calendar in [[Bizz_and_Finance|Business & Finance]]:

- For U.S. markets, "the year really begins in September": summer ends and traders are back at their desks.
- The last week of August has exceptionally low volume, often stretching past Labor Day until the September FOMC meeting.
- FOMC day is one of eight scheduled meetings a year and sees wild gyrations, especially just after the statement.
- Activity stays robust until Christmas, with a brief lull the day after Thanksgiving.
- The week between Christmas and New Year is quiet. Volume picks up after 1 January and stays seasonally strong until "Sell in May and Go Away".
- Activity hits its low the week after 4 July.
- Why it matters: low volume can produce unexpected moves that don't follow typical patterns.

## Surviving a system's normal losses

Protecting capital includes surviving the normal loss pattern of the chosen strategy rather than abandoning it after a familiar drawdown. Each system pays for its edge differently (from [[Markets - Trading]], via a [post by @CryptoCred](https://x.com/CryptoCred/status/2079138151528890777)):

- Trend-following pays through false starts.
- Momentum often enters late and can buy tops or sell bottoms.
- Mean reversion periodically gets overwhelmed by a genuinely outsized move.
- A recurring failure mode is abandoning a valid system because its unavoidable cost is mistaken for evidence that the system is broken.

See [[CryptoCred]] and [[Risk Analysis]].
