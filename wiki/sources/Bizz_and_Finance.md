# Business & Finance

Erlin's running note on money, markets and business. Most of it is pasted from X threads, a trading book and a shareholder letter, with a few short asides ("Look into commodity futures"). Handles in the note sit loosely beside the text, so a line is attributed below only where the note names its author plainly. Read it as a collection of takes, not verified claims.

## How markets react to news

GCR (@GiganticRebirth), 21 May 2021 (see [[GiganticRebirth]]):

> When news impacts the price, market participants often fixate on whether it's true or not true.
>
> More often than not, the actual veracity of the headline is immaterial.
>
> It's how the market reacts to the news, and for how long, that turns out to be a lot more informative.

A second take on timing:

> Markets are typically 6-8 months ahead of the news. Price moves first, and the "why" follows.
> Stocks peak when the best has already been discounted, with fundamentals often deteriorating later as expectations fail to match reality.
> "Stage analysis" helps in riding trends.

The "Everything is priced in" post, addressed to WallStreetBets and labelled "(Shitpost)" in the note:

> Don't even ask the question. The answer is yes, it's priced in. Think Amazon will beat the next earnings? That's already been priced in. … The market is an all powerful, all encompassing being that knows the very inner workings of your subconscious before you were even born. Your very existence was priced in decades ago … Free will is a myth. The market sees all, knows all and will be there from the beginning of time until the end of the universe (the market has already priced in the heat death of the universe).

## Valuation and bear markets

Equity valuation in three layers, from @MacroAlf citing "Great chart by Arbor Research":

| Layer | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| What's real | Book value |
| What's likely | Discounted earnings, next 3 years |
| Hopes & Dreams | Everything else |

> Way to go before Hopes & Dreams turn into Despair & Nightmares.

"Three Bear Phases", Robert Rhea (1935), via @WifeyAlpha:

> Bear markets seem to be divided into three phases:
>
> the first being the abandonment of hopes upon which the uprush of the preceding bull market was predicated;
>
> the second being the reflection of the decreased earning power and reduction of dividends;
>
> the third representing distress liquidation of securities which must be sold to meet living expenses.
>
> Each of these phases seems to be divided by a secondary reaction which is often erroneously assumed to be the beginning of a bull market.

The note adds: a secondary reaction is a bear-market rally. See [[Market Cycles]].

## The US trading calendar

A pasted passage from a trading book on US market seasonality. It explains why traders should care: low volume "can often lead to unexpected market movements that don't follow typical patterns". The excerpt breaks off mid-sentence.

| Period | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Last week of August | Exceptionally low volume, often stretching past Labor Day to the September FOMC meeting |
| September | "For the U.S. markets the year really begins in September." Summer ends, traders are back at their desks |
| FOMC meeting days | One of eight scheduled meetings a year; wild gyrations, especially just after the statement |
| September to Christmas | Activity and volume stay robust, with a brief lull the day after Thanksgiving |
| Christmas to New Year | Relatively low activity |
| January 1 to May | Volume picks back up and stays seasonally strong |
| May to summer | "Sell in May and Go Away" as the lazy days of summer approach |
| Week after July 4 | The year's low point for activity: trading desks "abandoned for lake cabins, golf courses, or seaside resorts" |

### Reflexive pumps

The note tags @diablofutur alongside this:

> Reflexive pumps don't begin with a wave of buyers, they start with no one left willing to sell. A stubborn holder base that refuses to let go triggers a synthetic supply shock. Aggro-rage shorts pile in. A disbelief rally erupts, fuelled by sideline capital waiting on a nuke that never shows. FOMO kicks in, chasing price into thin air, with zero marginal sellers and infinite shorts left to cover. Parabola and attention games begin.

### Perpetual futures

"[Perps](https://x.com/sershokunin/status/1945857878771818753) the best pmf in all of crypto…" The note then explains the idea's origin: in 1992 Robert Shiller proposed perpetual futures, together with a way to build asset-price indices by hedonic regression (dummy variables stand in for each element's unmeasured qualities, a form of repeated measures design). The aim was derivatives markets for illiquid, infrequently priced assets such as single-family homes, and for untraded indices and income flows such as labour costs or the consumer price index. See [[Crypto Trading]].

## Business strategy

### The catfish effect

An X [post](https://x.com/ruima/status/1937720853829157027) on a metaphor common in Chinese business strategy (鲶鱼效应) but rare in Western business schools. The story: Norwegian fishermen transporting sardines lost many because the fish stopped moving; one live catfish in the tank kept them swimming, and more survived. "The catfish made the environment slightly hostile, which weirdly made it more survivable."

- The post argues the idea caught on in China for cultural reasons: "more acceptance of discomfort as a developmental tool, and less hesitation to design pressure into the system from the top down."
- "It's not 'disruption' as a natural market phenomenon, it's more like managed agitation. Like, let's introduce a little stress on purpose, because stagnation is the bigger risk here."
- Tesla in China is the post's example. Frustrated with a domestic EV scene full of subsidy abuse and half-baked companies, the government gave Tesla full ownership of its China operations, fast-tracked factory deals, land and logistics support. "Let's put a catfish in the water." The post says innovation has picked up since, while granting it was not all Tesla.
- The contrast: in the US, more disruptive innovation; in China, competition "can be curated, introduced, even welcomed -- not to destroy the domestic players, but to get them moving again."
- It does not always work: "Sometimes the catfish is too strong, or the sardines were already too weak. And sometimes you get eaten, LOL."
- Its closing questions: "is that tank you're in the best environment for you? Are you swimming because you want to, or because you finally have to?"

Full page: [[Catfish Effect]]. Also [[Business Strategy]].

### Bezos: "Differentiation is Survival and the Universe Wants You to be Typical"

From Jeff Bezos's last annual shareholder letter as Amazon CEO. He quotes Richard Dawkins's *The Blind Watchmaker*: living bodies work constantly to stay different from their surroundings (warmer, wetter), and when that work stops at death they revert to equilibrium with the environment. Bezos takes it as a metaphor for companies, institutions and individuals.

> In what ways does the world pull at you in an attempt to make you normal? How much work does it take to maintain your distinctiveness?

> Democracies are not normal. Tyranny is the historical norm. If we stopped doing all of the continuous hard work that is needed to maintain our distinctiveness in that regard, we would quickly come into equilibrium with tyranny.

> The world wants you to be typical - in a thousand ways, it pulls at you. Don't let it happen.

> Being yourself is worth it, but don't expect it to be easy or free. You'll have to put energy into it continuously.

He illustrates it with a married couple's running joke ("Can't you just be normal?"), and closes: "The world will always try to make Amazon more typical - to bring us into equilibrium with our environment. … It will take continuous effort, but we can and must be better than that." See [[Jeff Bezos]], [[Entropy vs Extropy]] and [[Business Philosophy]].

### Deals, margins and negotiation

- "it's far better to buy a bussin company at a sus price than a sus company at a bussin price, fr fr"
- "All profit comes from margins."
- **Madman Theory.** A negotiation strategy used by Nixon during the Cold War: if your opponent believes you are irrational or unpredictable, perhaps capable of extreme actions, they may concede out of fear you will do something reckless.
- **Overearning and brand decay.** A thread replying to @buccocapital: "Feels like AirBnB is experiencing a meaningful degradation in their brand perception. They don't seem to be taking it very seriously...maybe they should?" Then: "Classic situation where fundamentals deteriorate with improving financials, because the company is overearning and consuming the entirety of consumer surplus. This is particularly acute in tech, where consumer surplus is usually subsidized for a decade by venture capitalists." And: "Well said. Often also an element of regulatory risk to these businesses as their strategy is to exploit legal loopholes to scale up/attempt to change policy once they're big enough".

### Double opt-in introductions

Before introducing two people, ask each privately whether they want the introduction and give enough context to decide. Connect them only after both agree. This protects attention, avoids social pressure, and makes a useful introduction more likely ([source](https://x.com/reidhoffman/status/2079177965766635936), _(@reidhoffman)_). The same consent boundary applies to agent-generated relationship suggestions in [[AI Agents]].

## Tokenization

A pasted reply thread on tokenizing assets. The pitch and the pushback, as the note has them:

**For.**

- "It's about creating liquidity in illiquid markets and opening up stock markets for 24/7 global trading." Candidates: real estate, IP, start-up shares.
- "today in 2025, owners of both public and private securities still trust away their assets to electronic databases managed by banks, broker-dealers, law firms -- or leave them idle on segregated cap table software or cloud-based spreadsheets". The cost of that siloed setup: "value extraction, mistake, and inefficiency across all stages of an asset's lifecycle", including costly trading fees.
- The real point is collateral value: borrowing against assets "in the world's largest lending pool" and posting them as collateral in other smart contracts, "making it more likely for people to buy them and less likely for them to sell them".
- Listed benefits: lending and borrowing through non-custodial protocols; trading 24/7/365 in a global market instead of US stock-market hours; selling to a broader pool of investors, including onchain users, organisations and protocol DAOs.
- "The assets that benefit the most from tokenization will be those that don't currently have a robust market structure. I would look beyond the most established, already liquid ETFs."
- "Why do you think Larry Fink is so interested in tokenization?"

**Against and in between.**

- "I get it for these friction-y things, altho prob these tokens would trade a massive premiums I would think but at least this solves something: it's a pain in the ass for most normal ppl to invest in those things".
- "No one cares. We WANT to outsource all this bs, esp for no fee and it's regulated by SEC so we sleep at noight"
- "Until you get debanked or account frozen due to your political speech online. Think Canadian truckers"
- "I can already order from the local Sears catalog just fine; why do I need this Amazon dot com thing?" (mocking the sceptics)

**On ownership.** "You don't own that. Your broker owns that on your behalf and you hold an account at your brokerage." And: "No, you don't own it in 5 seconds. Your investing app company will own it in T+1 days. And they will owe it to you. You will never own it. Next, try taking out a loan against your newly bought 'stock' on an open competitive market. Or send the stock to your friend in Yemen. If you really own it, that should be no problem."

## Money, consumers and incentives

### Women and consumer spending

"The [economy](https://x.com/bubblebabyboi/status/1961989514571751434) is propped up by women, they spend money like nobody else. I've been around billionaires who watch their dollars closer than the average 20 year old in a big city."

The note backs it with figures: women control or influence up to 85% of US consumer spending, some sources put global spending control at over $31.8 trillion, and women make most household purchasing decisions, especially for groceries, with major influence on new homes and cars. The note gives no specific source for the numbers. See [[Women]] and [[Economics]].

### Weird things happen at $0.00

> You can make things cheap and it will fix a lot of problems that will arise by making things free. Weird things happen at 0.00.

- **Singapore and the NHS.** Asked why Singapore does not provide free medical care, Ong Ye Kung answers: "when healthcare is free, and it's all paid for already by taxes, people have no discipline. The demand just booms." His example: an X-ray at the NHS takes nine months to a year, with seven million people on the waiting list, and "The NHS is crumbling under the demand."
- **Free community college.** San Francisco made its community college free, "And now apparently the slots get filled with scammers".
