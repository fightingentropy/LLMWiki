# Thoughts & Observations

Erlin's Thoughts note is a running file of short takes and pasted posts: lines in the note's own words sit next to posts copied from X, 4chan and YouTube comments. Where the note links a post or gives a handle, it is attributed below; other lines are as the note has them. Read it as a collection of takes, not findings. The later part of the note holds longer frameworks on intelligence, incentives and repeated games, each backed by an X post.

## Work, effort and attention

**One optional thing a day.** From a [post](https://x.com/skooookum/status/1932184434784010379) _(@skooookum)_:

> Compared to my 20s, I'm much more at peace with the fact that I only have the capacity to consistently do one optional thing really well each day. This is after all of my non-optional tasks every day (dog, relationship(s), work, chores, etc.). Acceptance is good because it helps me not revenge procrastinate, stress out about whether I'm doing enough, etc. I've got one thing, that's okay, and it's better to just do one thing each day than burn myself out indefinitely trying to do 3 or 4 things.

Acceptance prevents revenge procrastination and stress.

**Holidays leave a taste of freedom.** The note gives the handle @MazeDaMouth next to this:

> I have never come back from any holiday feeling relaxed, refreshed & reinvigorated to get back into work.
>
> I come back with the taste of freedom, still fresh in my mouth, a renewed hatred for work, and a strong suspicion that this is not what I should be spending my life doing.

Freedom shows what we actually want.

**The shadow economy of work.** From [@signulll](https://x.com/signulll):

> we’ve built an entire shadow economy around the simulation of work rather than work itself. the productivity-industrial complex feeds on the desire to feel like you’re making progress rather than actually making it.
>
> the demand for infinite guides, planners, frameworks, & self-improvement content isn’t surprising—there’s an underlying fear of inefficiency, of being left behind. but it’s kinda hilarious that the more you consume these meta-tools, the more you train yourself to exist in a preparatory state instead of an executory one.
>
> this isn’t new in human nature, but the internet has turbocharged it. there’s always more “research” to do before starting. always a more “optimized” method to consider. always a more “efficient” way to structure your to-do list. it’s a kind of paralysis disguised as momentum. go check youtube videos, holy shit.
>
> maybe that’s fine. maybe the human mind was always wired for this kind of recursive loop, & modern technology just exposed it. but it does feel like an intelligence trap—spending more time honing the system than actually using it.

The preparatory state replaces the executory state. See [[Self-Improvement Culture Critique]].

**Podcasts and passive learning.**

> Podcasts probably have the lowest information density of any medium ever created, but listening to them is a totally passive activity. Unlike reading, it requires no energy expenditure. That's partly why they're horrible for learning.

Passive consumption is not deep understanding.

**Death lies in banality.** The note gives the handle @rao_zam next to this:

> I've had to try and learn myself that death lies in banality. Accidents too. We risk so much for extremely small gains. We crash cars just because we couldn't wait to read a notification. But when you think about it, the trade deal is "I get to read this notification a few seconds earlier vs. I crash this car, maybe hurt myself and others and have to pay hundreds or thousands of dollars in repair".
>
> Exercise care. Especially when you're stressed.
>
> Running across the road because you're stressed or late for something is just never, ever worth it compared to what can happen.

Small stakes matter: the trade is seconds saved against a crash or death. See [[Decision-Making]].

## Opportunity, place and selection

**Serendipity and big cities.** A passage the note attributes to Taleb ([[Nassim Taleb]]):

> Many people do not realize that they are getting a lucky break in life when they get it.
>
> If a big publisher (or a big art dealer or a movie executive or a hotshot banker or a big thinker) suggests an appointment, cancel anything you have planned: you may never see such a window open up again.
>
> I am sometimes shocked at how little people realize that these opportunities do not grow on trees.
>
> Collect as many free nonlottery tickets (those with open-ended payoffs) as you can, and, once they start paying off, do not discard them.
>
> Work hard, not in grunt work, but in chasing such opportunities and maximizing exposure to them.
>
> This makes living in big cities invaluable because you increase the odds of serendipitous encounters-you gain exposure to the envelope of serendipity.

**Starlink and real estate.** The note gives the handle @welikejpegs next to this:

> @balajis is 100% right. "Starlink reprices the world." It's the most fundamental change to real estate mkts since in 50y+. On par w interstate highway system and watershed changes to US tax code in 1920s.

**Visible success reflects selection as well as merit.** Highly visible young winners are not selected by talent alone. Networks, sponsorship, access to capital, institutional incentives, narrative appeal and usefulness to powerful allocators can amplify one person while equally capable people stay unseen. The note frames this as a correction to pure meritocratic storytelling, not proof that young founders cannot attract capital or that every success is manufactured. ([post](https://x.com/robbezdjian/status/2083005594819027293) _(@robbezdjian)_)

**Leaving and staying.** A line and a comment on emigration and saudade:

As a Portuguese expat that has lived in the UK for more than 15 years, I am constantly finding that I left Portugal but Portugal never left me, quite the opposite. Abroad, the feeling of saudade just becomes more acute, more tangible. Being Portuguese is both a curse and a blessing, and I deeply love all that that entails. ## Intelligence, autism and AI

**Autism and integrity.**

> As I get older, I realize more and more that the best thing to do in life is to find autistic people and surround yourself with them
>
> the correlation between autism and traits like honor and integrity is astronomically high relative to the general population.

The reading: autists don't do social theatre; they do truth. See [[Neurodiversity]].

**The "exception" test, and its counterpoint.** A 4chan post saved in the note:

> The easiest and quickest way to test someone's intelligence is to make a generalized statement about a group.
>
> Say something like "asian men are shorter on average" if they say any variation of "not all asian men are short" or "I know a tall asian guy" you are dealing with someone genuinely low 10.
>
> They have proven that they aren't intelligent enough to understand abstract ideas like a statistical average or per capita. Why else would they think bringing up an exception like it was some profound statement? They literally CAN NOT understand abstracts, no attempt at rationalizing or explaining yourself will increase their IQ.

("low 10" is the note's text for "low IQ".) The point it keeps: an exception is not a refutation.

The note's later framework on outliers pushes back on the framing. An exception does not by itself disprove a population-level tendency, but a population average does not determine an individual case either. Ask whether the original claim is actually statistical, how large and well-supported the effect is, how much the groups overlap, and whether the decision concerns a population or the person in front of you. That framework keeps the principle and drops the insult-based framing of its source image. ([post](https://x.com/TheRabbitHole/status/2085792303533392157) _(@TheRabbitHole)_)

**High IQ and basic human situations.** A passage on Charlton saved in the note:

> Charlton (2009a, 2009b) argued that the reason why individuals with high IQ tend to fail in understanding and coping with basic human situations is that they try to apply complex and abstract thinking to problems for which instinctive strategies are more adaptive. Intelligence, he argues, is developed for evolutionarily novel problems and its application is maladaptive for situations for which natural selection has already provided the appropriate answers.

> -Why do intelligent people struggle with love? Because it’s not logical.

— [post](https://x.com/lokymann/status/1886166317381669170) _(@lokymann)_

**Ability is a bundle, not a scalar.** Analytical intelligence does not automatically supply competitive judgment, persistence, composure, social perception, or the willingness to act under pressure. The counterpoint: these are not mutually exclusive types, and unusually effective people may combine high reasoning ability, conscientiousness, emotional stability and competitive instinct. The posts' three-trait chart is an illustration, not sourced population evidence, and their inflammatory labels are discarded. ([post](https://x.com/iamgingertrash/status/2082860562355224922) _(@iamgingertrash)_; [post](https://x.com/lefttailguy/status/2082944239797940466) _(@lefttailguy)_)

**Maybe intelligence is not the limiting factor.** Clifford Sosin's thesis: abundant high-quality reasoning can transform work with smooth, verifiable solution spaces, but many important problems stay constrained by contact with reality. Ideas still have to be built, tested, measured and corrected; reality supplies new facts that reasoning alone cannot. ([post](https://x.com/CliffordSosin/status/2078594661359194500) _(@CliffordSosin)_)

**AI and human exceptionalism.** From [@signulll](https://x.com/signulll/status/1891192852711190634):

> The illusion of human exceptionalism crumbles fast when you see how much of our intelligence is just pattern recognition & predictive modelling. ai exposes how much of what we call “creativity” is remixing, how much “insight” is regurgitation, & how much “expertise” is just memorised heuristics.

**Asking AI to answer as an expert.** A comment saved under a [YouTube Short](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/rVlmbhwn0RM):

> This is actually spot on. I was asking AI (Gemini & ChatGPT) tech qusts about mechanical stuff for a particular car and kept getting answers I felt weren't accurate. I then asked them to answer my qusts as if they were a Volvo mechanic and the answers were hugely different and more in line with what I thought to be correct. Pretty amazing tbh. Deepseek on the other hand gave correct answers immediately

## Incentives, systems and repeated games

**Systems produce incentives.** A system that appears broken may be producing exactly the outcomes its incentive structure rewards. More effort inside the existing payoff matrix raises output without changing that matrix. Identify whose interests the structure serves, then either change the game or play the game that actually exists rather than optimising for the one you wish existed. ([post](https://x.com/incentivising/status/2081434700711596312) _(@incentivising)_)

**Environment must convert ability into returns.** Self-improvement can become an endless optimisation loop when the surrounding structure stays untouched. Becoming more skilled or disciplined does not guarantee a better outcome if the environment cannot reward those qualities. Ask which environment would convert what you already have into a return, what moving there would cost, and whether changing the structure now would beat another decade of polishing yourself inside the wrong one. ([post](https://x.com/incentivising/status/2082173497166446786) _(@incentivising)_)

**Win the repeated game, not every round.** Repeated interactions reward a strategy that begins cooperatively, responds promptly and proportionally to exploitation, and restores cooperation once the other side does. The durable lesson from Tit for Tat is not "dominate every encounter" but make cooperation profitable and defection predictably costly, while avoiding permanent revenge loops. The source essay dramatises Robert Axelrod's tournaments and overstates Tit for Tat as universally dominant; its performance depends on the population, noise, error correction and game structure, so treat it as a boundary-setting analogy, not a mathematical law for relationships. ([original essay](https://x.com/alexeixbt/status/2083512257808417082); [commentary and Prisoner's Dilemma framing](https://x.com/alexeixbt/status/2085402227489800240) _(@alexeixbt)_) The full synthesis is in [[Repeated Games]].

**Recovery depends on preserving later rounds.** After a major loss, the urge to erase it with one decisive gamble can destroy the only resource that makes recovery possible: continued participation. In an iterated game, stabilise first, cap further losses, preserve optionality, and plan recovery across later rounds instead of demanding that the present round repair the past. ([post](https://x.com/incentivising/status/2083985433973702656) _(@incentivising)_)
